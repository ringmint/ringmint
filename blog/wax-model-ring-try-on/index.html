What a wax model try-on is, and why every custom ring should have one.

By Chloe Alpert · September 7, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A wax model is a replica of your exact ring, cut in wax from the same file that will be cast in gold, and mailed to you to try on. It exists to answer the two questions a screen cannot: how the stone actually reads on that particular hand, and whether the size and proportions are right. It is the cheapest insurance in custom jewelry.

People agonize over carat weight on a screen. They compare a 1.5 against a 1.8 in renderings, read charts of millimeter spreads, and try to picture the difference. Then a wax model arrives, they put it on, and the decision takes about four seconds.

That gap between the screen and the hand is the entire reason wax models exist.

What is a wax model, exactly?

It is a physical replica of your specific ring, produced from the same digital file that will later be cast in metal. The name is not a metaphor. It is wax: the same castable wax the trade has used for a century, cut from your CAD file rather than carved by hand.

The important detail is not the material. It is that the model comes from the same CAD file that will be used for production. That is what makes it a proof rather than a mock-up. If the band on the model is 2.1 millimeters at the shoulders, the gold one will be 2.1 millimeters at the shoulders. The stone seat is where the stone will actually sit.

The model you are mailed is yours to keep. It is not the one that gets cast: when the design is approved, a fresh wax is cut from the same file, and that one goes into a flask, is surrounded by investment plaster, and is fired so the wax burns away and leaves a cavity in the exact shape of your ring. Molten gold goes into that cavity. Formlabs has a clear explanation of the lost-wax casting process. The technique is roughly 6,000 years old. Only the way the wax gets made has changed.

What does a wax model tell you that a render cannot?

Scale, profile, and fit. Three things a screen is structurally incapable of showing.

  • How big the stone actually reads on that hand. A render has no reference object. A 1.9 carat oval, which is now the average center stone size in The Knot's 2026 study, looks like a statement ring on a size 4.5 finger and looks restrained on a size 7.5. Same stone. Completely different ring. No amount of zooming tells you which one you are getting.
  • How high it sits. Profile height is the single most underrated dimension in ring design. A cathedral setting that looks elegant in a three-quarter view can be the ring that sits far higher off the finger than you pictured. Slipping the model on tells you that immediately, in a way no three-quarter render does.
  • How it fits and sits. Band width, the shape of the inside edge, and how the gallery sits against the neighbouring finger. People have strong opinions about this and almost none of them can predict those opinions in advance.

It is also a genuine sizing check. Sizing an engagement ring is harder than it sounds, especially when the ring is a surprise: GIA's ring size guide points out that the usual string-and-paper tricks are unreliable because string stretches, paper warps, and neither accounts for the knuckle the ring has to pass over. A model in the actual size settles it.

What can a wax model not tell you?

Being honest about the limits matters, because a model that is oversold disappoints:

What a wax model does and does not represent.
Accurate on the modelNot represented
Every dimension of the ringMetal color
Stone size and positionSparkle and light return
Profile height and band widthWeight in the hand
Finger size and fitSurface finish and polish
How it sits against another ringProng thickness once finished

The weight one surprises people most. Wax is close to weightless and gold is dense, so a finished platinum ring feels substantially more present on the hand than its model did. That is almost always a pleasant surprise, but it is worth knowing in advance.

Does a wax model actually change what people choose?

Most of the time, no. The design is already right, and the model says so. That is not a wasted step. Walking into a proposal certain about the ring is most of what the try-on is for.

When it does change something, the direction surprises people: they go smaller on the center stone, not larger. What happens is that the number stops being abstract. On a spreadsheet, 2.2 carats sounds better than 1.8 because it is a bigger number, and lab grown pricing means the jump often costs less than people assume. On the hand, the 1.8 sometimes just looks more like the person wearing it.

That is the whole case for the step. It usually costs you a week and buys certainty, and occasionally it catches the one thing a screen was never going to show you.

Does it add time or cost?

About a week, almost entirely mail time, and at Ring Mint there is no separate charge for it. Cutting the wax itself takes hours and the try-on takes a minute. The week is shipping the model out and getting your feedback back into a revised design.

If a proposal date is tight, this is the step that gets cut first, and cutting it is a real trade-off rather than a formality. It is worth building the extra week into the schedule from the start if you can. Our guide to how long a custom engagement ring takes lays out where that week fits.

Should you ask for one?

Ask if any of the following is true. Skip it if none of them are:

  • You are undecided between two stone sizes, or between two carat weights within about half a carat.
  • The ring has to sit flush against an existing wedding band.
  • The wearer works with their hands, or has said anything about rings catching on things.
  • You are guessing at the ring size rather than knowing it.
  • The design is unusual: an off-catalog shape, an heirloom stone, or an asymmetric setting.

If you are having a stock solitaire made in a size you have confirmed, a model is a nice-to-have rather than a necessity. Note that a wax try-on is only possible at all when a file of your specific ring exists, which is to say only on genuinely custom work. A configurator cannot print you a model of a setting that is already sitting in a warehouse.

One thing from the bench: it is real wax, and wax breaks. Try it on, look at your hand, take it off. The answer arrives in the first few seconds. Then keep it, or break it, it does not matter: the wax that gets cast is a fresh one, cut from the same file when the design is approved. Clients keep them more often than not.

Quick answers

What is a wax model ring?

A wax model is a physical replica of a specific custom ring, produced from the same CAD file that will be used to cast the finished piece. It is real castable wax, the same material the trade has used for a century, cut from your file rather than carved by hand. Ring Mint sends one on request, so the ring can be tried on and assessed before any metal is cast. Wax is fragile, so it is meant for a brief try-on rather than for wearing, and the client keeps it: a fresh wax is cut for the actual casting.

Is a wax model the same size as the real ring?

Yes. Every dimension is identical, because it is cut from the file that will be cast: stone size and position, band width, profile height, and finger size. What it does not represent is metal color, sparkle, weight, or final surface finish, since wax is close to weightless and does not reflect light the way polished gold does.

Does a wax model try-on cost extra?

At Ring Mint there is no separate charge for a wax model, and there is no concierge fee either. Practices vary across the trade, so it is worth asking any jeweler directly whether a model is included, billed separately, or not offered at all.

How much time does a wax model add to a custom ring?

Roughly a week, and it is almost all mail time. Cutting the wax takes hours and the try-on itself takes a minute. The week is shipping the model out and getting your feedback back into a revised design. If a proposal date is tight this is usually the first step to be cut, so it is better to build the week in from the start.

Can I get a wax model of a ring from a ring builder?

No. A wax model can only be made when a CAD file of your specific ring exists, which means the ring has to have been designed from scratch. Ring builders assemble stones into settings that already exist as finished stock, so there is no per-client file to cut a wax from.
