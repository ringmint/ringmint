How to design a custom engagement ring online, with an actual person helping you

By Chloe Alpert · September 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Designing a custom engagement ring online means working with Chloe on your ideas, stone selection and CAD design, with revisions and an optional wax try-on before production. You can start with inspiration pictures and questions.

You don't need to know how to design a ring to have one made.

I know that sounds obvious, but “custom” can make people feel like they are supposed to arrive with a drawing and the correct name for every little piece of metal, when really you might have a folder of screenshots and one very strong opinion about what you don't like.

Send me that. We can work with that.

At Ring Mint, designing a custom engagement ring online means working directly with me on the idea, the stone and the setting, reviewing the design in CAD, and having the option to try a wax model before the finished ring is made. There are decisions along the way. You get help making them.

Start with the parts you love

Maybe one ring has the right stone shape, another has the band you want, and a third has something going on from the side that you can't quite describe.

Point those things out. “This part” is a perfectly good description.

I also want to hear what bothers you. A setting looks too tall. A band feels too delicate. Everything you find seems a little too traditional, or the opposite, everything is so busy that the stone gets lost.

We can use references to understand your taste and develop an original design. You don't have to settle on a style label before we start.

There is a difference between designing a setting around your ideas and choosing combinations inside a ring builder. Both can be useful. If you want more on that distinction, I wrote a guide to custom versus customizable rings.

Bring the real-life details into it early

Before we get too attached to a design, tell me about the things it needs to work around.

Do you wear gloves for work? Do you already have a wedding band? Are you comfortable taking a ring off for activities, or are you looking for something that feels very easy to wear? Is the proposal a surprise, and do we actually know the ring size?

And tell me the budget and the date. Those aren't awkward details we have to tiptoe toward after looking at beautiful stones. They help define the project.

If the date is fixed, we need to discuss it before design decisions multiply. If the budget is fixed, we need to choose where it matters most to you.

A useful first message might be: “I like an elongated stone, I want yellow gold and a substantial band, I don't like high settings, and this is the budget I want to stay within.”

That's an example, not a form you have to fill out. Your own words are better.

Choose the stone and design together

I work with colored gemstones and natural and lab-grown diamonds, including colored diamonds. My family's particular specialty is colored stones, and our sourcing relationships date to the 1970s.

You may already know exactly what you want. You may only know the color.

Either way, the actual stone matters to the design. Its measurements, depth and shape affect the setting, and the setting affects the overall look of the stone. We can explore the design while sourcing, but the final geometry needs to work with the stone we are actually using.

This is also the point to be clear about material identity, natural or lab-grown origin, treatments and the documentation appropriate to the stone. A beautiful picture doesn't answer all of those questions. GIA explains what colored-stone identification reports can tell you.

CAD is where the idea becomes something we can examine

CAD means computer-aided design. It lets us look at the ring from different angles and work through its proportions before manufacture.

The top view matters, but please look at the side too. That's where you can see how the stone sits, what supports it, and how the ring might interact with a band beside it.

Some of the things to work through are:

  • Band width and how that width changes around the ring.
  • Stone position, orientation and setting height.
  • Prong or bezel details and how much of the stone they cover.
  • Space for another ring beside it.
  • Small personal details that may barely show from above.

A render is a design tool. It isn't a photograph of the finished piece, and it cannot promise exactly how a gemstone will look in your kitchen or in daylight. Use it to understand the design, then use actual stone images and the right questions to understand the stone.

This is the time to say what you think

If something looks wrong to you, say it.

You don't have to diagnose the design problem. “The band looks heavier than I pictured” gives us somewhere to start. So does “I like it from the top, but the side feels too busy.”

The purpose of reviewing a custom design is to get the decisions into the open before production. Talk through the revision process and what is included in your project, and make sure the agreed design is the one you intend to approve.

Sometimes a change affects something else. Lowering a stone may change wedding-band clearance. Making a band finer may affect what details it can accommodate. Those are the conversations that turn a collection of preferences into a ring that works.

You can try a wax model

Ring Mint offers an optional wax model so you can try the shape on your hand before production.

That can be helpful when a dimension on a screen doesn't mean much to you, you can look at a measurement and nod along and still have no real sense of whether you will like that amount of ring on your finger until there is something there.

Wax can help you assess scale and shape. It won't show the weight of the finished metal or the sparkle and color of the actual stone. I explain those differences in the wax model try-on guide.

The personal detail doesn't have to be obvious

One engagement ring upgrade we made had a 3ct marquise diamond in 18k yellow gold. Inside the band were four small stones representing the husband, wife and their two children.

From above, you see the marquise. The family detail is tucked inside.

The customer's response included, “Exactly what I was picturing.” You can see the ring and her message in the marquise upgrade story.

I love that example because it shows how specific custom can be. A personal detail doesn't have to take over the design. It can be something you know is there.

Approval, production and the questions to settle first

Before moving into production, be clear on the chosen stone, metal, ring size, design and agreed terms. Ask what the timeline includes and what happens if you want to change something after approval.

Production time is only one part of the full project. Sourcing, decisions and revisions happen before that. We have a separate guide to how long a custom ring takes, and your own timeline needs to be discussed for your piece.

You should also read the current order, returns and warranty terms. Keep the practical details in writing so you know what to expect.

Your first message can be simple

Tell me what you love, what you can't find and what you want to spend. Send the screenshots. If there is a ring you already wear and want to work around, send that too.

You don't need to make all the decisions before contacting me. Making them together is a large part of the work.
