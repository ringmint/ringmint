<p>You find the color you love, but the band is too thin. Then you find a setting that feels right, but the stone is the wrong shade. Then you find something that looks promising and the description raises more questions than it answers, and suddenly you have spent three evenings looking at rings and you're less sure than when you started.</p>

<p>This is a very reasonable point to talk about custom.</p>

<p>A mint-green engagement ring can start with the color and the feeling you want, then we can work through the actual gemstone, its suitability for your life, and the setting around it. You don't have to find all three already assembled in a listing.</p>

<h2>First, show me your mint green</h2>

<p>Mint, seafoam, pale green and blue-green overlap in everyday language. They don't describe one exact color or one gemstone.</p>

<p>Show me the reference you keep returning to. Is it almost blue? Is it soft and grayish? Is there a yellow tone you love, or one you really don't want?</p>

<p>And is the thing you love actually the whole combination, the pale stone against a white metal, the shape lying across the finger, the thick band around it? It helps to know that, because we could find a similar color and still build a ring that doesn't feel anything like the picture in your head.</p>

<p>You can be very particular about this. We're discussing a ring you want to wear.</p>

<h2>Start with possibilities, then examine actual stones</h2>

<p>Tourmaline is one place to explore for a green stone. Sapphire is another. A green lab-grown diamond is also worth considering. Depending on the shade you mean, we may want to compare other materials too, but I would rather look at a manageable set of relevant options than give you a list of every green gemstone on earth.</p>

<p>The important thing is to compare the actual candidates. A gemstone name doesn't guarantee the exact shade, brightness or proportions you want.</p>

<p>My family specializes in colored stones and has global sourcing relationships dating to the 1970s. That is experience we can bring to a brief like this. We can talk about the stone and the ring together, rather than asking you to make one choice without understanding how it affects the other.</p>

<h2>Tourmaline and sapphire involve different wear considerations</h2>

<p>If mint tourmaline is the look you love, we need to discuss its care along with its color. GIA lists tourmaline at 7 to 7.5 on the Mohs hardness scale, with fair toughness. Sapphire is harder, at 9, and GIA describes its toughness as excellent. Hardness concerns scratching; toughness concerns resistance to breaking or chipping. Neither number means a ring is immune to damage. <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/tourmaline-care-cleaning" rel="noopener">Tourmaline care</a>, <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/sapphire-care-cleaning" rel="noopener">sapphire care</a>.</p>

<p>That doesn't make the decision automatic. It makes your habits relevant.</p>

<p>If you want to wear your ring constantly and rarely think about taking it off, say so. If you already remove jewelry for hands-on activities, tell me that too. The actual stone's condition, treatment and setting also need to be considered.</p>

<p>For tourmaline, GIA recommends warm soapy water and advises against ultrasonic and steam cleaning. Get care instructions for the specific finished piece rather than assuming the cleaner you use for another ring is appropriate here. <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/tourmaline-care-cleaning" rel="noopener">GIA's cleaning guidance</a>.</p>

<h2>What about a green lab-grown diamond?</h2>

<p>Yes, absolutely. Diamond is an obvious option to discuss when we are thinking about a ring for everyday wear. Its scratch resistance is a real advantage: diamond ranks 10 on the Mohs scale. It can still chip or fracture, so the cut, setting and care still matter. <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/diamond-care-cleaning" rel="noopener">GIA explains diamond durability here</a>.</p>

<p>The harder part can be the green itself. In my sourcing experience, finding that amazing, rich green in a lab-grown diamond is much harder, and then of course we also want the right shape, proportions and a stone that works within your budget, so there are a few things we are trying to bring together.</p>

<p>But we've done it.</p>

<p>If that is the color your heart is set on, tell me. Rich green and pale mint aren't the same brief, and I want to see which one you mean before we start looking. We can explore actual options and be clear about the stone's lab-grown origin, any color treatment and its documentation. Finding it before doesn't mean the same shade is always available, but it is absolutely a conversation worth having.</p>

<h2>Be careful with “lab-grown mint tourmaline”</h2>

<p>This is a description I would want to investigate before making a buying decision.</p>

<p>A listing needs to tell you what the material actually is. A pale-green laboratory-grown stone isn't automatically tourmaline, and “lab-grown” and “looks like” are different claims. GIA defines a synthetic gem by its correspondence to the natural material's chemical, optical and physical characteristics. <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/gem-synthetic" rel="noopener">GIA's guide to synthetic gem materials</a>.</p>

<p>Ask for the exact material identification and supporting documentation. Don't infer it from the color, the product title or a seller's use of the word “lab.”</p>

<p>If lab-grown origin matters to you, tell me at the beginning. Then we can explore documented options and be honest about what is available. I wouldn't want you choosing a stone under one description and discovering later that you bought something else.</p>

<h2>An east-west setting changes the whole feeling</h2>

<p>Turning an elongated stone across the finger can be a useful starting point if you want a ring that feels broader or less like the traditional upright solitaire.</p>

<p>But “east-west” is only an orientation. We still need to decide how much metal you want around the stone, how wide the band should be, and how the ring should look from the side.</p>

<p>For a substantial, gender-neutral design, we might explore an elongated stone with a bezel, a wider band, or a more sculptural shape. Those are possibilities to work through, not rules about who a ring is for.</p>

<p>You can like a soft green stone and want a strong, simple setting. You can want curves without wanting something delicate. Tell me the combination you mean.</p>

<h2>Low profile, chunky metal and wedding-band clearance</h2>

<p>These are three separate design questions, and they affect each other.</p>

<p>A deep stone needs somewhere to go. A low setting may occupy the space where a straight wedding band would otherwise sit. A wider band may need a different sizing conversation from the thin ring you currently wear.</p>

<p>A bezel can offer protection around a stone's edge, but it does not make the gemstone unbreakable. We also need to think about what that border does visually. How much metal do you want to see, and does the stone still have the presence you wanted once we put it inside that frame?</p>

<p>This is the sort of thing we can work through in CAD, and an optional <a class="text-link" href="/blog/wax-model-ring-try-on/">wax try-on</a> can help you understand the scale on your hand. The wax won't show the gemstone's color. It helps answer a different question: do you like this shape as a ring?</p>

<h2>Look at the stone outside its best photograph</h2>

<p>Ask for views in more than one lighting environment, with enough movement to understand what you're seeing. A stone can look different as the lighting, viewing angle and surroundings change, and your screen introduces another variable.</p>

<p>The goal isn't to find a gemstone that looks identical in every situation. It's to understand the one you are choosing.</p>

<p>If the only view you have is one bright close-up, ask for more information before getting attached. That is especially useful when your whole brief depends on a subtle shade.</p>

<h2>Decide what you are flexible about</h2>

<p>Suppose you want a very particular pale green, an elongated shape, a low bezel and a substantial white-metal band. That's a useful brief. Add your budget and timing, and we can start discussing feasibility.</p>

<p>Then think about what you would change first if the exact combination is difficult to source. A slightly different proportion? A nearby shade? A different material, provided you understand what it is?</p>

<p>You don't need to answer instantly. These are the decisions we can talk through as actual options emerge.</p>

<h2>Send me the ring you can't find</h2>

<p>Send the color reference, the setting you like and the part that has been frustrating you. “I love this stone but I would never wear that band” tells me quite a lot.</p>
