            <p>Nearly every jeweler in the world advertises custom engagement rings. The word has been stretched so far that it now covers three completely different products, and the person buying a ring has no way to tell which one they are being sold until the ring shows up.</p>
            <p>Here is the version I would give a friend. There is one question that separates the three, and it is not about price, service, or how nice the website looks. <strong>It is whether a mold for your ring already exists.</strong></p>

            <h2>What a ring builder actually does</h2>
            <p><strong>A ring builder is a filter over an existing catalog.</strong> You pick a setting from a menu, pick a stone from a list, and the site shows you a rendering of the two together. That is genuinely useful. It is also not design.</p>
            <p>Everything in a builder was designed before you arrived. The prong count, the band width, the basket shape, the gallery underneath, the height the stone sits at, the way the shoulders taper: all decided, all fixed, all already cast in a mold that will be used for hundreds of other rings. Those are two separate things a builder locks down at once, incidentally. GIA's <a href="https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/" class="text-link" rel="noopener">guide to ring settings</a> draws the distinction well: a <em>setting</em> is how the stone is held (prong, bezel, channel), while a <em>style</em> is the arrangement of the whole design (solitaire, halo, three stone). Your choices are the ones the menu offers, usually metal, center stone shape, center stone size within a range, and sometimes a band width or a hidden halo toggle.</p>
            <p>The industry term for this is <strong>semi-mount</strong>. The setting is a finished product waiting for a center stone. When a large online retailer says "build your own ring," a semi-mount is what they mean, and there is nothing dishonest about it. The dishonest part is when a small jeweler with the same catalog calls it bespoke.</p>

            <h2>What fully custom actually means</h2>
            <p><strong>Fully custom means the setting is modeled from nothing, in CAD, for one person.</strong> There is no menu, because there is no catalog to draw from. The starting point is a conversation, and the first thing that exists is a file that did not exist yesterday.</p>
            <p>What that changes in practice: the band can be any width at any point along its length. The stone can sit at any height. The basket can be shaped around a specific finger, or around a specific wedding band that has to sit flush against it. A side stone can be a family diamond that is 4.1 millimeters when every catalog setting expects 4.0. The gallery can carry an engraving nobody else will ever see.</p>
            <p>None of that is exotic. It is just what happens when the geometry is drawn to fit rather than picked from a list.</p>

            <h2>What about semi-custom?</h2>
            <p><strong>Semi-custom is a real category and it is where most "custom" jewelers actually operate.</strong> An existing setting is modified: a prong style swapped, a band narrowed, a halo removed. The mold exists, but it gets altered before casting, or the CAD file for a stock design is edited rather than drawn fresh.</p>
            <p>This is a legitimate middle ground and it is often the right answer. It is faster than fully custom and cheaper than most people assume. It just has a ceiling. You can move a design a certain distance from where it started, and past that distance, editing an existing file becomes slower and more error-prone than drawing a new one.</p>

            <h2>Is a custom engagement ring more expensive?</h2>
            <p><strong>Usually not, and often the opposite.</strong> This surprises people, so it is worth explaining where a ring's money actually goes.</p>
            <p>In a typical engagement ring, the center stone is 50 to 70 percent of the total, the metal and the labor of casting and setting are most of the rest, and the design work is a small slice of what remains. Drawing a setting from scratch adds hours of CAD time, not a different order of magnitude of cost. Meanwhile the thing that genuinely inflates a retail ring price is overhead: a lease, a sales floor, inventory that has been sitting in a case, and staff commission. A ring that has been sitting in a display case has been paid for by the store, insured by the store, and marked up to cover the months it did not sell.</p>

            <h2>When is a ring builder the right choice?</h2>
            <p><strong>When you like a stock setting and you need the ring quickly.</strong> I mean that sincerely. If you have found a solitaire in a builder that looks exactly right, and the proposal is in nine days, buying it is the correct decision and paying more for a custom version of the same silhouette would be a waste of money.</p>
            <p>A builder is also the better call if the appeal for you is seeing the price update in real time as you change the stone. Custom does not work like that; the quote comes after a conversation, and some people find that uncomfortable. That is a real preference and it is worth respecting.</p>
            <p>Where a builder fails is narrower than it sounds, but it fails completely. It cannot set a stone that is not a standard size. It cannot make a band that curves around another ring. It cannot make something that is not already in the catalog. If any of those describe what you want, no amount of filtering will get you there.</p>

            <h2>How to tell which one you are being sold</h2>
            <p>Four questions, and the answers are hard to fudge:</p>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>"Will I see a CAD render of my actual ring before it is made?"</strong> A builder shows you a stock rendering with your stone dropped in. Custom shows you a file of your ring, from any angle, with your measurements on it.</li>
              <li><strong>"Can I change the band width at the shoulders but not at the back?"</strong> A deliberately awkward request. Fully custom is a yes without hesitation. A configurator cannot do it and a semi-custom jeweler will tell you it depends on the base design, which is an honest answer.</li>
              <li><strong>"How many revisions do I get?"</strong> A fixed number usually means someone is editing a stock file and counting billable hours.</li>
              <li><strong>"Can I try it on before it is cast?"</strong> A mailed <a href="/blog/wax-model-ring-try-on/" class="text-link">wax model</a> is only possible when a file of your specific ring exists.</li>
            </ul>

            <p class="aside">One thing from the bench: the request that most often forces a truly custom build is not an exotic design. It is an inherited stone. Old stones are cut to old proportions and almost never land on a modern standard size, so a catalog setting either does not close around them or holds them badly. That is when "customizable" runs out of room.</p>

Q: What is the difference between a custom and a customizable engagement ring?
A: A customizable ring, usually called a ring builder or a semi-mount, pairs your chosen center stone with a setting that already exists in the jeweler's catalog. Your choices come from a fixed menu: metal, stone shape, stone size, sometimes band width. A fully custom ring has no catalog behind it. The setting is modeled from scratch in CAD for one client, so every dimension is decided rather than selected.
Q: Is a custom engagement ring more expensive than a ring builder?
A: Usually not. The center stone is 50 to 70 percent of a ring's cost and the metal and labor are most of the rest, so drawing a setting from scratch adds hours of CAD work rather than a different order of magnitude of cost. What actually inflates retail ring prices is overhead: a lease, a sales floor, held inventory and commission. A custom jeweler with no store typically comes in 30 to 50 percent below the retail price of a comparable ring.
Q: What is a semi-mount?
A: A semi-mount is a finished setting made and stocked without a center stone, waiting for one to be set into it. Ring builders are built almost entirely from semi-mounts. It is a legitimate product and often the right one, but it means the design existed before you did.
Q: Can a ring builder set an heirloom diamond?
A: Only if the heirloom happens to match a size the setting was made for. Older stones are cut to older proportions and rarely land on a modern standard measurement, so a stock setting either will not close around them or holds them insecurely. Resetting an inherited stone is one of the most common reasons a ring has to be built from scratch.
Q: How do I know whether a jeweler is offering true custom?
A: Ask whether you will see a CAD render of your actual ring rather than a stock rendering, whether you can change one dimension in isolation (the band width at the shoulders but not at the back, for instance), how many revisions are included, and whether you can try a wax model on before anything is cast. Only a ring modeled from scratch can answer all four.
