<p>You don&#x27;t need to explain why the rings you&#x27;ve been shown don&#x27;t feel like you.</p>

<p>Maybe you like a substantial band but don&#x27;t want something heavy. Maybe you want a diamond, just not on a very fine band. Maybe you love a delicate ring and are tired of being told that isn&#x27;t the category you should be shopping in.</p>

<p>I would rather you show me what you like.</p>

<p>We can talk about the shape, the stone, the metal, how much presence you want and what you need to be able to do with your hands all day. Those are things we can design around. A label doesn&#x27;t tell me nearly as much.</p>

<h2>What makes an engagement ring gender-neutral?</h2>

<p>For this conversation, gender-neutral means we are not assigning a ring design to a particular gender. It does not mean the ring has to look one particular way.</p>

<p>A plain band can be an engagement ring. So can a sculptural setting, a diamond solitaire, a colored-stone ring or something with a very quiet detail that only you know is there. Substantial and minimal are options, not requirements.</p>

<p>If the phrase helps you find designs you like, use it. If you would rather just call it your engagement ring, that works too.</p>

<p>The useful starting point for <a class="text-link" href="/custom-engagement-rings/">a custom engagement ring</a> is what you want to wear, rather than which department it would sit in.</p>

<h2>How do you start when you don&#x27;t have a finished design?</h2>

<p>Bring a few references and tell me which parts you like. They do not all need to agree with each other.</p>

<p>You might like the band on one ring, the stone orientation on another and the finish on a third, and then have a picture of something you absolutely don&#x27;t want. That is useful information. We can pull the ideas apart before trying to put them together.</p>

<p>I would start with questions like these:</p>

<ul><li>Do you want the stone to be the main thing you notice, or the whole shape of the ring?</li><li>Do you like visible metal around the stone?</li><li>Would you rather the ring feel quiet, substantial, sculptural or somewhere between those?</li><li>Are you planning to wear another ring beside it?</li><li>What jewelry already feels comfortable on your hand?</li></ul>

<p>You do not need technical language for any of this. “This part feels too fussy” tells me something. So does “I love it, but I would catch it on everything.”</p>

<p>Our guide to <a class="text-link" href="/blog/design-custom-engagement-ring-online/">designing a custom engagement ring online</a> explains how those first references become a design we can actually review together.</p>

<h2>How wide should the band be?</h2>

<p>The right band width depends on your hand, the rest of the design and the way you want the ring to feel. There isn&#x27;t a universal gender-neutral measurement.</p>

<p>Width is only one dimension. Two bands with the same width can feel quite different if one is thicker, if their edges are shaped differently or if the inside profile is different. A photograph from above will not tell you all of that.</p>

<p>I would also want to see how the band and the stone relate. A broad band with a smaller stone might give you the balance you love. A larger center stone might work with a band that has a different shape through the shoulders. We need to look at the combination.</p>

<p>If you are used to a thin ring and considering something much wider, try a comparable width before settling the size. Comfort is worth checking on your own hand; I would not guess a fixed size adjustment from a photograph.</p>

<p>And if you like a fine band, say so. You don&#x27;t have to make the ring wider to qualify for this conversation.</p>

<h2>Is a low-profile setting always the best choice?</h2>

<p>A lower setting is worth exploring if you want less height above the finger, but we need to check it against the actual stone and your wedding-band plans.</p>

<p>The stone has depth underneath the part you see. That space has to go somewhere. Asking for the lowest possible ring, a deep stone and a straight wedding band that sits tightly beside it may create competing requirements.</p>

<p>This is why a side view matters so much. I want you to understand where the stone sits, what is underneath it and how another ring would meet it, rather than choosing from the prettiest angle and hoping the rest works out.</p>

<p>A fitted wedding band is one possible direction. A deliberate gap might be perfectly fine with you. A different setting height could be the better choice. There isn&#x27;t one answer until we know what matters most.</p>

<h2>Bezel or prongs: which suits the design?</h2>

<p>A bezel surrounds the stone&#x27;s edge with metal; prongs hold it at selected points. The choice affects the look of the ring and how the stone is supported.</p>

<p>A bezel can give a ring a more continuous outline and provide protection around the edge. Prongs can give a more open appearance. Neither is a guarantee against damage, and the quality and condition of the finished setting still matter. <a class="text-link" href="https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/" rel="noopener">GIA&#x27;s guide to engagement ring settings</a> explains the basic constructions.</p>

<p>What interests me here is how those choices serve your ring. Do you love a frame around the stone? Do you want to see more of its outline? Are there pointed corners we need to think carefully about protecting?</p>

<p>We can discuss protection without turning it into a rule that every practical ring must look the same. Maintenance and occasional checks belong in the conversation whichever direction you choose.</p>

<h2>Can we turn the stone east-west?</h2>

<p>An elongated stone can be explored across the finger rather than along it, but we should check the actual dimensions and setting before deciding.</p>

<p>An oval, emerald-cut or marquise has a very different relationship to the band when you rotate it. The width across your finger changes, the silhouette changes, and the proportions that looked right in one orientation may need adjusting in the other.</p>

<p>This is where I come back to something I say a lot: carat is weight.</p>

<p>In <a class="text-link" href="/blog/custom-colored-gemstone-sourcing/">our stone-sourcing guide</a>, I show two stones side by side. The smaller-looking one weighs 2.94ct; the larger-looking one weighs 2.84ct. That is why I want us looking at millimeter measurements and proportions alongside weight, especially when we are deciding how a stone should sit across your hand.</p>

<p>The goal is the ring you want to see and wear. A number alone can&#x27;t show you that.</p>

<h2>Does the center stone have to be a diamond?</h2>

<p>No. We can explore a diamond or a colored gemstone, with the practical conversation matched to the actual material.</p>

<p>I work with natural and lab-grown diamonds as well as unusual colored stones. My family&#x27;s jewelry background goes back to 1979, and colored gemstones are a particular part of that history. If you have a very specific color in mind, I want to see it.</p>

<p>I also want to know how you plan to wear the ring. Loving a stone and understanding its care needs can exist in the same conversation. I am not interested in talking you out of something meaningful to you just because another choice would be easier, but I do want you to have the information.</p>

<p>An opal is an example I would approach carefully for everyday engagement-ring wear. GIA describes its relatively low hardness and vulnerability to damage from heat or abrupt temperature changes. If your heart is set on one, those limitations need to be understood before we discuss the setting and care. <a class="text-link" href="https://www.gia.edu/opal-care-cleaning" rel="noopener">GIA&#x27;s opal care guidance</a> explains the concerns.</p>

<p>A setting cannot make every gemstone suitable for every routine.</p>

<h2>How do you make the ring personal without adding more decoration?</h2>

<p>Personal meaning can live in a small detail. It does not have to change the whole appearance of the ring.</p>

<p>For one actual <a class="text-link" href="/blog/marquise-engagement-ring-upgrade/">marquise engagement ring upgrade</a>, we included four hidden stones inside the band to represent the husband, wife and their two children. The visible design and the private meaning were both part of the piece.</p>

<p>I am sharing that as an example of a personal detail, not labeling the client&#x27;s ring or identity. Your version could be entirely different.</p>

<p>Maybe there is a color you associate with someone. Maybe the meaningful choice is the stone itself. Maybe you want the outside of the ring to stay very simple. We can talk through what is feasible without adding something just for the sake of calling it custom.</p>

<h2>What should I check in the CAD and wax try-on?</h2>

<p>Use the design stages to check proportions, height and comfort, not only whether the image looks beautiful.</p>

<p>A CAD rendering helps us look at the proposed construction from different angles. A <a class="text-link" href="/blog/wax-model-ring-try-on/">wax-model ring try-on</a> can help you understand the shape on your hand, with the limitation that wax does not have the weight, finish or appearance of the completed metal and stone.</p>

<p>Look at the width through the band. Look at how high the setting sits. Think about neighboring fingers and any ring you want to wear beside it. If something feels too tall or too broad, that is worth discussing while we are still making design decisions.</p>

<p>You don&#x27;t need to protect my feelings by pretending you like a proportion you are unsure about. Tell me what you are seeing. That is the work.</p>

<h2>What should I send you to get started?</h2>

<p>Send a few references, your budget range and your timeline. If you know your ring size, include it. Tell me what you like wearing now and anything you already know you don&#x27;t want.</p>

<p>If you and your partner are both choosing rings, you don&#x27;t have to want the same thing. We can talk about a shared detail if that matters to you, and leave room for each ring to feel right on its own hand.</p>

<p>What I want is for you to look at the finished design and recognize yourself in it. Not feel as though you picked the closest available option and made peace with the rest.</p>
